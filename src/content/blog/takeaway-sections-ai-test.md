Three posts on this site are research pieces, not personal incidents:
whether llms.txt does anything, whether schema markup moves AI citations,
how much AI Overviews cut organic clicks. All three are accurate. All three
cite real, checkable studies. I still had to go back and rewrite the ending
of every single one.

## The test

The question that caught it: could someone who didn't experience this
specific thing today have written this sentence? If yes, cut it or make it
more specific.

Applied to the body of those three posts, almost everything passed — the
data, the studies, the numbers are all real and specific, not something a
generic AI-content site would produce from nothing. Applied to the endings,
almost nothing passed.

## What it actually caught

Every one of the three posts had built up real, specific momentum through
the body and then closed with a section literally titled "The takeaway" —
and every one of those sections read like the kind of paragraph that closes
a thousand other SEO blog posts, regardless of who wrote the research above
it. Here's the actual line that used to close the schema markup post:

<div class="callout">
<p class="eyebrow">before</p>
<p>"Schema markup isn't doing anything harmful, and there are legitimate reasons to keep using it... But the evidence doesn't support treating it as the lever that gets a page cited by AI."</p>
</div>

Nothing wrong with it, exactly. It's also a sentence any other site running
the same Ahrefs study through the same summarizer could have produced. It
doesn't know it's on my site specifically.

## What replaced it

The fix wasn't cutting the ending — it was dragging it back to a specific
decision instead of a general conclusion:

<div class="callout">
<p class="eyebrow">after</p>
<p>"I'm leaving the FAQ schema already on both my sites in place... I'm not adding more of it as an AI-citation play, and I'm not counting the checklist item as done just because the markup exists."</p>
</div>

Same underlying finding. The difference is that the second version only
makes sense coming from someone who actually has two specific sites with FAQ
schema already on them, deciding something specific about what to do next —
not a summary anyone else's AI assistant could have handed back from the
same source material.

## Why this happened three times in a row

Research posts don't come with a built-in personal stake the way a "my
sitemap broke" post does. The body stays anchored to real sources because
the sources are doing the work. The ending is where a draft defaults back to
generic advice-column voice, because summarizing a topic is exactly the kind
of task language models are built to do well — including mine.

## Where this leaves things

The test is in the content playbook now, in the section it belongs to,
specifically so I don't skip past a draft's ending without rereading it
closely next time. That's a narrower fix than "write more authentically" —
it's a habit for one specific weak point in one specific step of how these
posts get made.
